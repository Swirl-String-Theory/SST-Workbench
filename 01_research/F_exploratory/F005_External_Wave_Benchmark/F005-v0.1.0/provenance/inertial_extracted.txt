                                                                                        translating through a rotating fluid

                                                                                        Results of an experimental study investigating issues of Coriolis effects on the fluid
                                                                                        dynamics associated with vortex rings propagating through a rotating fluid are presented.
                                                                                        The vortex rings are generated at the top of a large, water-filled rotating tank and they
                                                                                        propagate downwards along the axis of rotation. The motion and the decay of the rings in
                                                                                        a rotating fluid are expected to be accompanied by an inertial-wave field being established
                                                                                        in the fluid surrounding the rings. However, the existence of this inertial-wave field had
                                                                                        previously never been verified experimentally. Particle image velocimetry measurements
                                                                                        were performed with the goal of demonstrating the existence of the inertial-wave field.
                                                                                        Datasets were processed to extract individual inertial-wave modes and, for the first
                                                                                        time, experimentally construct the dispersion relation for the inertial waves associated
https://doi.org/10.1017/jfm.2026.11199 Published online by Cambridge University Press




                                                                                        with vortex rings. For rotation rates when Coriolis forces dominate the dynamics, the
                                                                                        experimental data are found to be in very good agreement with the well-established
                                                                                        theoretical dispersion relation for inertial waves. The generation of inertial waves implies
                                                                                        that kinetic energy is radiated away from the vortex rings. First results relating to the
                                                                                        redistribution process of the kinetic energy are briefly discussed.

                                                                                        Key words: vortex dynamics, rotating flows, waves in rotating fluids



                                                                                        1. Introduction
                                                                                        Vortex rings represent a generic flow structure frequently studied to elucidate fundamental
                                                                                        aspects of vortex dynamics. Early theoretical research on vortex rings dates back to, at
                                                                                        least, the classic study by Helmholtz (1858). The first, detailed experimental investigation
                                                                                        was performed by Kaden (1931). Studies on vortex rings propagating through a
                                                                                        non-rotating fluid are abundant in the literature – for comprehensive overviews the reader

                                                                                        is referred to, for instance, Shariff & Leonard (1992), Akhmetov (2009) and Danaila,
                                                                                        Kaplanski & Sazhin (2021). However, investigations on vortex rings in a rotating fluid
                                                                                        are very scarce.
                                                                                           The current study addresses aspects of the dynamics of vortex rings arising from
                                                                                        Coriolis forces affecting vortex rings developing in a fluid that is subject to background
                                                                                        rotation. The particular case considered here is that where the vortex rings concentrically
                                                                                        enclose the axis of rotation of the system and propagate along the direction of that axis.
                                                                                        As far as we are aware the only two existing experimental studies that have previously
                                                                                        addressed this flow scenario are those by Verzicco et al. (1996) and Brend & Thomas
                                                                                        (2009). One may expect vortex-ring motion in a rotating fluid to be accompanied by the
                                                                                        generation of inertial waves within the ambient bulk fluid. The results presented here focus
                                                                                        on the inertial-wave field surrounding such vortex rings propagating and decaying subject
                                                                                        to background system rotation.
                                                                                           Inertial waves are a particular type of mechanical wave that can be generated in rotating
                                                                                        fluids (cf. Davidson 2013). Inertial waves are internal waves. That is, they propagate within
                                                                                        the fluid rather than at its surface. Inertial waves arise as a consequence of the Coriolis
                                                                                        force representing a restoring force that tries to return displaced fluid to its equilibrium
                                                                                        position. The Coriolis force (per unit volume) is given by
                                                                                                                             FC = −2ρ Ω × u,                                      (1.1)
                                                                                        where ρ is the density of the fluid, Ω is the angular velocity vector and u is the velocity
                                                                                        vector. As a consequence of the cross-product in (1.1) the Coriolis force always acts at
                                                                                        a right angle to the direction of motion and that is the reason for it acting as a restoring
                                                                                        force. The cross-product also leads to the peculiar characteristic that the phase velocity of
                                                                                        inertial waves is perpendicular to their group velocity, the latter being a measure of the
                                                                                        rate and direction of energy transfer. The dispersion equation for inertial waves is given by

                                                                                                                                                   k·Ω
                                                                                                                       ω = ±2Ω cos θ = ±2Ω               ,                        (1.2)

                                                                                        is associated with an inertial wave with a unique frequency ω. In particular, since |cos θ| ≤

                                                                                           The only existing study addressing the issue of inertial waves in connection with vortex
                                                                                        rings propagating along the axis of rotation in a rotating fluid was performed by Verzicco
                                                                                        et al. (1996). They conducted a combined experimental and computational programme.
                                                                                        Key results of their investigations were that moderate levels of background rotation lead to
                                                                                        a decrease of the translation velocity of the rings. Concurrently a structure with opposite
                                                                                        circulation was observed to form ahead of the ring, accompanied by the generation of
                                                                                        an intense axial vortex on the axis of symmetry in the tail of the ring. They moreover
                                                                                        observed that, when rotation rate is sufficiently high for the motion to be dominated by
                                                                                        Coriolis forces, then vortex rings hardly formed. Subsequently Brend & Thomas (2009)
                                                                                        performed experiments complementing those of Verzicco et al. (1996). They determined
                                                                                        an estimate for the decay length that vortex rings can travel along the axis of rotation
                                                                                        for successively increasing rotation rates. That is, the distance that rings can travel until
                                                                                        they have fully lost their structural integrity. In the context of their study, Brend & Thomas
                                                                                        (2009) also briefly addressed the flow structure ahead of the vortex rings and in their wake,
                                                                                        when the rings are subject to background rotation.

                                                                                           The experimental particle image velocimetry (PIV) data of Verzicco et al. (1996) were
                                                                                        not sufficient to yield experimental evidence of the presence of inertial waves in the
                                                                                        flow field surrounding the vortex rings. However, together with their experimental data
                                                                                        they discuss computational simulations and identify contours of azimuthal vorticity about
                                                                                        which they write that ‘it is tempting to identify these structures as inertial waves which
                                                                                        allow the ring to radiate energy’ (Verzicco et al. 1996, p. 233, lines 3–4).
                                                                                           Based on the results of Taylor (1922), Verzicco et al. (1996), Scase & Terry (2018) and
                                                                                        Crowe, Kemp & Johnson (2021), the existence of inertial waves surrounding the vortex
                                                                                        rings has to be expected – of course. The contours of azimuthal vorticity for vortex rings in
                                                                                        Verzicco et al. (1996) are visually similar to the theoretical streamline contours by Taylor
                                                                                        (1922) who considered inertial waves for the related case of a rigid sphere propagating
                                                                                        through a rotating fluid along the axis of rotation. Similarly, the theoretical analyses of
                                                                                        Scase & Terry (2018) and Crowe et al. (2021) indicated an inertial-wave field surrounding
                                                                                        Hill’s spherical vortex in a rotating fluid moving along the axis of rotation.
                                                                                           Nevertheless, a definitive experimental confirmation of the existence of the inertial-
                                                                                        wave field in the liquid ambient to the vortex rings requires experimental data suitable for a
                                                                                        direct comparison with the theoretical dispersion curve of (1.2). However, at the time of the
https://doi.org/10.1017/jfm.2026.11199 Published online by Cambridge University Press




                                                                                        study of Verzicco et al. (1996), over thirty years ago, the experimental PIV methodology
                                                                                        and the required image processing procedures, requiring the collection and analysis of
                                                                                        very large digital datasets, were not advanced enough to isolate individual inertial-wave
                                                                                        frequency modes in the flow field together with their associated propagation angles with
                                                                                        respect to the axis of rotation (cf. Verzicco et al. 1996, p. 234, lines 3–5). Thus, it was not
                                                                                        possible for Verzicco et al. (1996) to produce experimental data revealing clear evidence of
                                                                                        the presence of inertial waves. Therefore, it was also impossible to produce experimental
                                                                                        data enabling a direct comparison with the theoretical dispersion equation (1.2). That is,
                                                                                        experimental data revealing how the frequency of individual inertial-wave modes relates
                                                                                        to their associated propagation angle. Thus, the purpose of the current study was to use
                                                                                        modern, state-of-the-art data collection and image and data processing methodologies to
                                                                                        close that gap.

                                                                                                                     3.2. Visualisation of inertial waves
                                                                                        As outlined in § 1 the overriding goal of the study was to confirm the existence of the
                                                                                        inertial-wave field in the flow region surrounding the vortex ring by isolating different
                                                                                        frequencies through appropriate filtering of the PIV data of the VLS plane and thereby
                                                                                        becoming able to experimentally reproduce the dispersion relation given by (1.2). Samples
                                                                                        of visualisations of inertial waves will be displayed. This is done separately for the PIV
                                                                                        data sampled in the VLS and the HLS planes identified in figure 2(b). For both the VLS
                                                                                        and the HLS data, it is demonstrated that the inertial waves can be identified independently
                                                                                        in each of the two velocity components associated with the two perpendicular PIV data-
                                                                                        sampling modes. In § 3.4.2 the filtered VLS data are used to demonstrate that the data
                                                                                        follow the theoretical dispersion curve of (1.2).

                                                                                        3.2.1. Data processing to isolate inertial-wave modes from PIV data of VLS plane
                                                                                        The dispersion curves for the inertial waves can only be reconstructed from experimental
                                                                                        PIV velocity data obtained in the VLS plane of figure 2(b). That is because, according
                                                                                        to (1.2), the different inertial-wave modes of frequencies ω rely on the angle θ between the
                                                                                        axis of rotation and the direction of the particular wave mode.
                                                                                           Thus, the inertial waves are associated with velocity components u and v in the
                                                                                        directions of, respectively, the x and y coordinate axes in figure 2(b). The PIV data for the
                                                                                        velocity components u and v were analysed separately to demonstrate that the theoretical
                                                                                        dispersion curves can be reconstructed experimentally from both velocity components
                                                                                        independently. The data-analysis process is briefly outlined by referring to the velocity
                                                                                        component u, on the understanding that the velocity component v was independently
                                                                                        subjected to the corresponding process.
                                                                                           In order to extract the different wave modes, it is required that the PIV velocity data
                                                                                        are filtered for the particular mode that is to be identified. The data analysis proceeded
                                                                                        successively for all of the p × q = 149 × 239 velocity-data points for sequences of video
                                                                                        recordings. In preparation for the filtering process the mean-flow component u( p, q),
                                                                                        averaged over all velocity-data points p, q and all video frames, was initially calculated.

                                                                                        (1/2π) ω = (1/2π) 2Ω = (1/π) × 1.26 rad s−1 ≈ 0.40 Hz.
                                                                                           The number of samples determines the number of frequency bins that the fast Fourier
                                                                                        transform can allocate. The number of frequency bins represented the largest issue
                                                                                        associated with the data analysis. The maximum frequency of the inertial waves is ω = 2Ω
                                                                                        1029 A47-8
                                                                                                                        Journal of Fluid Mechanics
                                                                                        and the number of frequency bins within this range depends on the rotation rate of the
                                                                                        rotating turntable. For instance, a rotation rate of 12 rpm corresponds to a rotational
                                                                                        velocity of 2π × 12 rpm/60 s = 2π/5 rad s−1 . This corresponds to a rotational frequency
                                                                                        of 1/5 Hz, such that the maximum inertial-wave frequency is 2 × 1/5 Hz. For a recording
                                                                                        of length 60 s, at a recording rate of 60 frames per second, there are 3600 samples. Thus,
                                                                                        the number of frequency bins is 1800 while the Nyquist frequency is 30 Hz. Consequently,
                                                                                        the width of each bin is 30 Hz/1800 = 1/60 Hz. Therefore, the number of bins in the
                                                                                        inertial-wave range is (2/5) Hz/(1/60)Hz = 24 bins. Since the maximum inertial-wave
                                                                                        frequency decreases linearly with the rotational frequency of the rotating tank so does the
                                                                                        number of bins. That implies very low numbers of bins at lowest rotation rates. Increasing
                                                                                        the recording length to increase the number of samples, and therewith the number of
                                                                                        bins, cannot resolve the issue. That is, because the vortex rings decay, and due to viscous
                                                                                        dissipation, the strength of the signals decay. Thus, the issue outlined above represents
                                                                                        a natural limit associated with identifying inertial waves associated with the motion of
                                                                                        vortex rings in a system subject to background rotation. Nevertheless, the data to be
                                                                                        presented display good agreement with the theoretical dispersion curve for the highest
                                                                                        rotation rates used and where vortex rings still existed for sufficiently long as a coherent
                                                                                        structure before disintegrating due to the destabilising effects (cf. Brend & Thomas 2009)
                                                                                        of the background rotation.

                                                                                        When collecting PIV data in the HLS plane (see figure 2b) the associated velocity
                                                                                        components are the v and the w component in, respectively, the y and the z coordinate

                                                                                        it is more convenient and useful to work in polar coordinates r , φ. Therefore, the v and
                                                                                        w velocity components were transformed into velocity components u r and u φ in the y−z
                                                                                        plane. Here φ is the polar angle between locations in the HLS light sheet of figure 2(b)
                                                                                        around the x-coordinate direction. Due to the underlying symmetry of the flow geometry

                                                                                        the velocity component v for the VLS data in § 3.2.2. Of course some deviations from the
                                                                                        symmetry can naturally develop as the rings propagate downwards, away from the nozzle
                                                                                        exit.

                                                                                        individual inertial-wave modes of figures 4 and 5 have to be imagined as cones of opening
                                                                                        angle θ , where the x axis represents the vertical centreline of the cones. Thus, when

                                                                                        in the vertical and horizontal directions have been calculated, the data for equal distances,
                                                                                        in the vertical and horizontal directions, are averaged. That is, for instance, the correlations
                                                                                        for all unique combinations of data-point pairs in the vertical direction that are separated by
                                                                                        a distance of, say, ten data points are averaged to yield one mean value for that separation

                                                                                        distance. A corresponding averaging is conducted for all other data-point separations in
                                                                                        the vertical direction. The analogous process is then carried out for all possible separation
                                                                                        distance in the horizontal direction. The mean values obtained in that way are then
                                                                                        normalised by the mean value of the correlation coefficient obtained for autocorrelations at
                                                                                        all data-point sites. That is, by correlations for the cases where dh = 0 and dv = 0. Sample
                                                                                        data obtained by the process outlined above are displayed in figure 9. The results displayed
                                                                                        in the figure are for a vortex ring at Ro = 1.59 and Re = 10 000, where the original velocity
                                                                                        field was filtered for frequencies corresponding to waves emitted at angles 60◦ (0.2 Hz)
                                                                                        (figure 9a,b) and 45◦ (0.2828 Hz) (figure 9c,d). Note that our Python code was set up to
                                                                                        separately process the data to the left and to the right of the axis of symmetry (axis of
                                                                                        rotation). This approach was adopted to avoid the correlation reaching a secondary peak
                                                                                        where the radial distance would reach across the axis of symmetry. Since a wave field
                                                                                        is considered the value of the mean correlation data will oscillate between positive and
                                                                                        negative values as one intersects across different phases. In an unbounded inviscid system,
                                                                                        one would expect the oscillations to continue with constant amplitude. However, in a real
                                                                                        fluid with viscous dissipation the magnitude of the oscillations will reduce, and this is
                                                                                        reflected by the data in figure 9.

                                                                                        4. Conclusion
                                                                                        The primary purpose of the experimental study summarised here was to verify the
                                                                                        existence of an inertial-wave field associated with vortex rings translating along the axis
                                                                                        of rotation through a rotating fluid. The existence of such a wave field has to be anticipated
                                                                                        a priori. Its existence is also expected on the basis of the computational data for vortex

                                                                                           However, there has hitherto not been an experimental confirmation of the existence of
                                                                                        the inertial-wave field. The only existing experimental studies on vortex rings propagating
                                                                                        through a rotating liquid along the axis of rotation are those by Verzicco et al. (1996) and
                                                                                        Brend & Thomas (2009). The latter publication did not address the issue of inertial waves.
                                                                                        Verzicco et al. (1996) did carry out PIV measurements that were conceptually very similar
                                                                                        to those conducted as part of the current study. Nevertheless, their study was performed
                                                                                        over 30 years ago. At the time the technological methodologies to collect and process
                                                                                        the very large datasets as required to confirm the existence of the inertial-wave field by
                                                                                        filtering velocity PIV data for individual wave modes and perform extensive correlations
                                                                                        to obtain data for comparison with the theoretical dispersion curve of (1.2) were not yet
                                                                                        available.

                                                                                        of the PIV data sampled in the VLS plane of figure 2(b).

                                                                                        subjected the available PIV data to further analysis to obtain some first quantitative
                                                                                        information as regards the energy redistribution associated with the formation of the
                                                                                        inertial waves. It was found, as expected, that for vortex rings propagating in a non-rotating

                                                                                        of the nozzle diameter until the rings undergo laminar–turbulent transition. Thereupon,
                                                                                        turbulent mixing results in a broadening of the region containing the bulk of the energy.

                                                                                        radially outwards as far as four nozzle diameters. The data collected that demonstrate this

                                                                                        existing dependence of the energy redistribution process on the Reynolds number was not
